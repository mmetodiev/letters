title: "Every Age Thinks It's the Worst One"
--- 

I want to push back on something I hear often, and probably say myself more than I should: the idea that things are especially bad now, that the particular corruption we're living through is historically unusual, that previous generations had more integrity or more shame or more of whatever quality seems most lacking at present.

They didn't. I've been reading enough history to say this with some confidence. The scandals that define our era — the bribes, the performative virtue alongside private vice, the powerful protecting each other while the rules apply selectively to everyone else — these are not new. There were trials in Rome where the jury was bought and paid for, where the currency of exchange was not just money but access to women and young men, where the defendant in an adultery case secured his acquittal by committing further adultery with the people deciding his fate. This happened in the presence of men whose names we still use as synonyms for uprightness. The corruption didn't wait for virtue to leave the room. It operated right alongside it.

> All ages will produce men like Clodius. Not all ages will produce men like Cato.

The road to vice runs downhill and steep, and most people don't need a guide to find it — they arrive there easily, almost naturally, carried along by proximity and habit and the example of everyone around them doing the same. This isn't pessimism. It's just accurate. What's harder, what always requires more effort and more deliberate cultivation, is the other direction.

But here's what I find genuinely consoling, and I want you to hold onto it: even the most corrupted people know. That's the telling thing. Nobody commits their worst acts in public if they can avoid it. The fraud is hidden. The betrayal is rationalized. The cruelty is given a better name. Even people who have long since stopped trying to be good retain enough of the original orientation to be ashamed — or at least to prefer that no one is watching.

This means the knowledge hasn't been destroyed. It's been suppressed, covered over, overridden by habit and appetite and self-interest. But it's there. Conscience is the proof of it.

Here is the point that stays with me: the first and worst punishment for doing wrong is having done it. Not the external consequences — those can be avoided, and often are. The internal one, which can't. Because guilt doesn't require a verdict. It requires only the guilty person to be alone with themselves.

I've watched people who have done serious harm move through the world apparently untroubled. And maybe some of them genuinely are. But most of them — and I think this is observable if you watch carefully — carry a low continuous anxiety, a vigilance that never quite relaxes, a need to keep certain thoughts from forming all the way. Fortune can free a person from punishment. It cannot free them from fear.

> Good luck frees many men from punishment, but no man from fear.

This isn't justice, exactly. It's something more like a structural feature of having a conscience you can't fully silence. The crimes that escape every external reckoning still have to be lived with. And that living-with is its own sentence, served quietly, without ceremony, for as long as the person lasts.

So no — the age isn't especially bad. It's just our turn to be inside it. Which means our task is the same as it's always been: to be among those who make the other choice, and to make it repeatedly, in the small moments that nobody's recording, because that's where character actually forms.
