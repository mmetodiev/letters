There was a night recently — good food, better conversation, someone read something aloud that made the whole room go quiet in the best way. I thought of you immediately. I wanted you there. I'm writing this instead.

What was read was old. Written by someone dead for two thousand years who somehow knew exactly what the room needed. That keeps happening to me, and I've stopped being surprised by it.

> The great ones worked without knowing your name, and yet somehow for you specifically. That's what makes them worth the reverence.

I've been thinking about what we owe the past — not as debt, but as relationship. I keep company with certain writers the way you keep company with people you trust. Not to agree with everything, not to become them, but to be in the presence of something that took a whole life to build. You come away from it larger. That's the test.

But here's what Seneca adds that I find quietly thrilling: the work isn't finished. Everything they discovered, every hard-won clarity, every mapped territory of the mind — it's an inheritance, not a conclusion. We're supposed to add to it. The person born a thousand years from now should find the account larger than we left it.

That means you. The thinking you do, the way you work something out and then hand it to someone else — that's the continuation. Not original in the sense of having no sources, but original in the sense of being genuinely yours.

> Receive what was given. Increase it. Pass it on. That's the whole of the philosopher's task, and it belongs to anyone willing.

I am still learning from you. I want you to know that.

Yours, at a table I wish you'd been at —
