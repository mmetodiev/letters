title: "Gold Leaf Over Ugly Wood"
--- 

I want to say something about surfaces, because I think we're all more deceived by them than we admit — including me, including you.

Look at the men who walk through the world with their heads high, carrying titles and wealth and the deference of crowds. Now look beneath. Not with malice — with accuracy. What's under the gold leaf? Often: anxiety about losing it, contempt disguised as ambition, the hollow feeling that comes from spending a life acquiring things that turn out not to be the thing. The marble is a veneer. We know this about buildings, and we pretend not to know it about people.

We were trained early to mistake the gleam for the substance. Our parents meant well — they wanted us safe, provided for, respected — and they communicated this want as reverence for money, which is what they'd been taught too. The lesson goes in before we can question it, and by the time we're old enough to examine it, it's already part of how we see. Poverty looks like failure. Wealth looks like proof of something. These are not small errors; they shape everything downstream — who we marry, what work we do, how we spend our hours, what we pray for.

> All ask how great my riches are, but none whether my soul is good.

I'm quoting a playwright because the line is too exact not to. It was hissed off the stage — the audience recognized themselves and couldn't stand it. The playwright stood up and asked them to wait for the ending, to see what the gold-chaser's life actually cost. That's the only experiment worth running. Not whether wealth arrives, but what it does to the person it arrives in.


Here is what I've observed: the agony of possessing is greater than the agony of acquiring. We suffer getting there; we suffer harder once we've arrived, because now there is something to lose, and loss is always coming, and we know it. Every new gain merely raises the floor of what feels like enough. The man you envy for his prosperity is gaping after the next thing, already dissatisfied with what he has, already measuring himself against what he doesn't. This is not an accident. This is the structure of the project itself.

Greed is wretched in what it craves, and wretched in what it wins. There is no version of this game where the player ends content.


What philosophy offers — the real thing, not the performance — is simpler and harder than any of this: the absence of regret for your own conduct. Not happiness as the world defines it, not the satisfaction of having accumulated enough, not the relief of being admired. Just this: a soul that holds its own order, that is pleased with itself for the very things that displease others, that measures its progress by how free it is from craving and from fear.

This cannot be given to you by clever words or elegant argument. It can't be worn like expensive clothes or displayed like a well-furnished house. It has to be built from the inside, slowly, by actually examining what you value and why, by repeatedly choosing the real thing over its gleam.

> Let words proceed as they please, provided the soul keeps its own sure order.

That's the whole of it. Everything else is decoration.
