title: "What the Crowd Does to You"
---

I want to tell you something I'm embarrassed to admit: I never come back from large gatherings the same person I was when I entered. Something I had quieted gets noisy again. Something I had routed finds its way back. I bring home a version of myself that is louder, more competitive, more easily provoked — as if the crowd had its own atmosphere and I had been breathing it without noticing.

This is not weakness that will disappear when I've made more progress. It is a fact about how character works. Even the strongest swimmers are moved by a strong enough current. The question is not whether crowds affect you — they do, they affect everyone — but whether you know it and account for it.

I went to a public spectacle recently, expecting something light. What I found was the pure form of what crowds produce when given permission: cruelty, enjoyed openly, and escalating. By the afternoon the audience was no longer watching violence — it was directing it, demanding more, unsatisfied by anything short of complete destruction. I watched the faces around me and saw something that unsettled me more than the arena: how quickly the ordinary person becomes an ordinary monster when surrounded by other ordinary monsters doing the same thing.

> Much harm is done by a single case of indulgence. The crowd doesn't ask you to change; it simply changes you while you're not looking.

The remedy is not misanthropy — hating the many because they are unlike you is its own kind of contamination, and a lonelier one. The remedy is selectivity. Withdraw into yourself as far as you can. Choose companions who will make you better, and welcome those you can make better in return. The work runs in both directions; we teach by learning and learn by teaching.

And here is what I want to leave you with — three sayings that arrived together and belong together:

*One man means as much to me as a multitude, and a multitude only as much as one man.*

When they asked a philosopher why he applied such effort to work that would reach so few, he replied: *I am content with few, content with one, content with none at all.*

And from Epicurus, writing to a friend: *I write this not for the many, but for you; each of us is enough of an audience for the other.*

This is what I mean. The approval of crowds is not a measure of anything worth measuring. If the many understand you easily, ask yourself whether you've said anything that cost you something to say. Your good qualities should face inward. The only audience that matters is the one who knows what you're actually doing — and for that, one person is enough.

You are enough. I write this for you.

Yours, and not the crowd's,
