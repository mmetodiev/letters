title: "The Cart, the Figs, and the Blush I Couldn't Hide"
--- 

I want to tell you about a trip I took recently, because something small happened that I haven't been able to stop thinking about.

A friend and I traveled simply — by choice, not necessity. One bag each. Basic food. No performance of comfort. And it was genuinely good. The kind of good that's hard to arrange on purpose but arrives naturally when you stop carrying so much. We ate dried figs and talked and I read more than I do at home, and at the end of two days I felt more like myself than I had in weeks.

But here's the thing I have to admit: when we passed other travelers — people in nicer vehicles, with attendants, with the visible signals of someone who has arrived — I felt a flush of embarrassment. Not shame about what I was doing. Embarrassment about being *seen* doing it. The blush came before I could stop it. And that involuntary redness told me something true: the ideas I claim to hold haven't finished making their way into my body yet. You can believe something and still flinch. The flinch is the honest measure.

> He who blushes at riding in a rattle-trap will boast when he rides in style. I haven't fully escaped that bargain.

What I keep returning to is the question of what we actually own versus what we're accessory to. There's a particular kind of person — you've met them — who is, in every meaningful sense, poorer than they appear. The estates, the accounts, the elaborate retinue: all borrowed against a future that may not deliver. Strip out the debt and there's less than nothing. We call this person wealthy because we've agreed to count the trappings instead of the substance.

The same confusion runs deeper than money. Riches don't corrupt people by direct action — they corrupt through what they do to the imagination. They puff something up in the spirit. They create a false sense of size, a posture of greatness without its content. Arrogance is what you get when the appearance of greatness arrives without the thing itself. And the appearance travels faster and requires less work, which is part of why it's so compelling.

Here's what I've come to think, and I'll spare you the syllogisms I've been turning over, because the syllogisms are less useful than just saying it plainly: good doesn't grow from bad soil. Not because of a logical rule, but because of how things actually work. A fig tree doesn't produce olives. Character doesn't emerge from the conditions that corrode it. If you want to know what a person is actually worth, look past what they hold and ask what holds them.

The wise person isn't greater because of what they possess. They're greater because of what they've grown in themselves — something that doesn't fluctuate with fortune, that doesn't depend on the approval of other travelers on the road. A soul with genuine size doesn't need the cart to signal it.

I said I'd spare you the syllogisms, and I will. But let me say this about them honestly: they're a way of avoiding the harder thing. If you want to live more simply, live more simply. If you want to be free of what wealth does to the spirit, practice being free of it — not in argument, but in the daily choices that no one's watching. The logic that proves riches aren't a good does nothing for the person who can't stop wanting them. Only living differently does that.

This is what my blush was telling me. I knew the argument. My face hadn't caught up.
