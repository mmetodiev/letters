title: "You Are What You've Digested"
--- 

I've been traveling more than usual lately, and I notice it does something good to my thinking — not despite the movement but because of it. There's a laziness that settles into the body when you stay in one place too long, and it creeps into the mind too. A change of road shakes something loose. I read on these trips, maybe more than at home, and I've been thinking about what reading actually does — what it's *for*, and what it isn't.

It isn't accumulation. That's the mistake I made for years, and I suspect you've made it too: treating what you read as inventory. Books finished, ideas catalogued, passages underlined and never returned to. The mind full but not nourished. There's a difference between a stomach that holds food and a body that's been fed.

The bees understand this better than most readers do. They don't hoard what they collect — they *transform* it. What comes out of the hive bears no obvious resemblance to any individual flower. It's something new, made possible by everything that went into it, but not reducible to any one source. That's the model. Not a filing cabinet. A digestive system.

> We should so blend what we've absorbed that it becomes clearly our own — even when its origins can still be faintly traced.

What you've read should become part of how you think, not a library you consult. The test isn't whether you can cite the source. It's whether the idea now lives in you — whether it shapes the way you see things on an ordinary Tuesday, without you having to reach for it.

This is why I'm wary of people who read only to repeat. They can tell you what everyone thinks; they have no thoughts of their own. And I'm equally wary of people who write without reading — who confuse the noise in their own head for originality. The real thing is rarer: a mind that has taken in widely, digested thoroughly, and then speaks in its own voice. Not a chorus of citations. Not a sealed room. Something more like what happens when many voices sing together and what emerges is a single sound that none of them could have made alone.

You don't achieve this by trying to sound like no one. You achieve it by absorbing so many influences, so fully, that they stop being separable. A child resembles their father without being their father. That's the relationship to aim for with the writers who've shaped you — familial, not photographic.

Here's what reason keeps telling me, and I pass it on not because I've mastered it but because I need to hear it said out loud: the things we run toward — status, approval, the kind of busyness that signals importance — these are distractions from the one work that actually compounds. A mind that has learned to think clearly, to absorb and transform and speak truly, keeps getting better. Everything else either levels off or decays.

The road to that is quieter than it sounds. Less impressive from the outside. No threshold crowds, no anxious comparisons with whoever is ahead or behind you. Just the daily work of reading something worth reading, sitting with it long enough for it to change shape inside you, and then — eventually — finding that it comes out as yours.

> It is a rough road that leads to the heights of greatness; but if you scale it, you proceed to the top over level ground.
