title: "What Stays After the Lecture Ends"
--- 

I want to tell you about a teacher I had when I was young, because I've been thinking about what it means to actually learn something — not to have heard it, not to have written it down, but to have taken it into the body so completely that it changes how you live.

His name was Attalus. I used to arrive before anyone else and leave last. I would wait for him in the corridors and challenge him to discussions as he walked, because I couldn't get enough. When he spoke about the uselessness of luxury, I wanted to go home poor. When he spoke about the body's unnecessary demands, I simplified my eating and my sleeping almost immediately. Some of those habits have stayed with me for fifty years. Not because I decided to preserve them as a tribute to him — because they became mine.

That's what I mean by learning something.

The lecture hall fills with many kinds of people, and most of them are not there to be changed. Some come for the pleasure of the language, the way you might go to a concert. Some come to collect sentences they can repeat at dinner. Some arrive with notebooks and leave with transcriptions — words perfectly preserved, person entirely untouched. And there's nothing wrong with any of this, exactly, except that it mistakes the container for the thing.

> He who walks in the sun, though he walk not for that purpose, must needs become sunburned.

Philosophy works the same way — by proximity and repetition and gradual absorption, not by a single dramatic encounter. You sit near it long enough and it gets into you whether you're trying or not. But only if you're actually sitting near it, actually letting it make contact, not holding it at arm's length as an object of study.

Here's the distinction I want you to hold: there's the person who approaches a text as a scholar, noting the word choices and the historical context and the metrical patterns and the sources — and there's the person who approaches it as someone who needs what it contains. Same text. Entirely different activity.

The scholar reads *time flies away and cannot be restored* and notes that Vergil uses *fugit* rather than *cedit*, and tracks the usage across the corpus, and compares it to earlier poets. The philosopher reads the same words and thinks: I am wasting time right now. What am I doing with this day? These are not the same reading, and only one of them costs you anything.

I don't say the scholarly reading is worthless. I say it's a different project, and a safer one — because it never requires you to change.

What Attalus gave me, and what I'm still trying to give you, is not information. It's a way of being oriented. The ideas are the same ideas that have been available for centuries — they're not secret, they don't require a particular teacher. What requires a teacher, or a letter, or a repeated encounter with the living tradition, is the ignition. The moment when the general truth becomes specific enough to apply to you, right now, in the life you're actually living.

Words must become deeds. This is the whole of it. Everything else — the arguments, the syllogisms, the careful distinctions — is scaffolding. At some point the building has to stand on its own.

I've been talking to you long enough that you already know most of what I'm going to say before I say it. That's fine. Knowing isn't the same as remembering at the right moment. And remembering at the right moment isn't the same as acting on it. Keep going.
