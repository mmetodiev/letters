title: "The Voice You Need in the Room"
--- 

Here is something I've come to believe: most of what we think is original in ourselves arrived from outside. The fears that feel most intimate, the ambitions that feel most personal, the particular shape our restlessness takes — very little of it came from within. It was absorbed. Poured in through proximity, through example, through the accumulated pressure of what everyone around us values and chases and worships. We are, to a degree that should unsettle us, the residue of other people's opinions.

This is why I keep returning to the question of whose voice you're carrying. Not the voice you know you're carrying — the people you've consciously learned from, the books you've read deliberately, the ideas you've chosen to adopt. I mean the ones that got in before you were paying attention. The ones that told you what a good life looks like, what's worth wanting, what you're allowed to expect. Those voices are still talking. They're talking right now, underneath whatever you think you're thinking.

The only answer I know to this is to have a countervailing voice — something steady that you've chosen and returned to often enough that it has some real weight against the noise. Not a set of rules. Rules are easy to forget or work around. Something more like a presence: a way of thinking that you've practiced until it can actually push back against what the crowd is saying. Philosophy, at its best, works this way. Not as a system you understand but as a habit of attention you've developed — a kind of immune response to the infections that come with ordinary social life.

> Vices are not inborn in us; they have come from without, heaped upon us. Nature produced us in health and freedom.

I find this genuinely consoling, and I want you to as well. You were not born this way. Whatever in you that chases the wrong things, that mistakes status for substance, that quiets its own better judgment when the room disagrees — that came from somewhere. Which means it can, with effort, be displaced by something else.

Here's what I've noticed about advice, including the advice I give you: it doesn't work the way medicine works, where you take the dose and the illness retreats. It works more like repeated small corrections to a course. You hear something true, and it registers, and then the noise comes back and covers it over, and then you hear it again, and it registers again, a little deeper this time. The accumulation matters. This is why I keep writing.

You already know most of what I tell you. You know that the admired men toppling from their positions looked happy from the outside and felt terrified on the inside. You know that the pursuit of power leaves the pursuer the most harried person in any room. You know that wealth measured by what you need is a different thing entirely from wealth measured by what you've accumulated. You know these things. But knowing them and having them ready — having them close enough to use when the moment arrives — are different conditions, and the gap between them is where most of us live.

Look at Alexander, who moved through the known world taking everything, and was himself taken — not by a foe but by his own appetite. Look at every figure history offers as an example of power: the pattern repeats without variation. At the height of what looked like success, privately undone. Driven not by choice but by compulsion, like something falling that can only stop when it hits the ground. These aren't cautionary tales. They're just accurate descriptions of what happens when no countervailing voice ever gets through.

> You need never believe that a man can become happy through the unhappiness of another.

Be someone who has a voice that tells you the truth — and return to it often enough that it has a chance against everything else. That's what I'm trying to be for you. That's what I ask you to find, in whatever form serves you best.
