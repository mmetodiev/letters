chapterNum: 90
title: "What We Had Before We Wanted More"
--- 

There's an argument I keep returning to, and I want to share it with you because I think it changes how you see the present.

Somewhere in the deep past — before accumulation became the organizing principle of a human life — people lived in genuine sufficiency. Not abundance in the modern sense, not luxury, but something rarer: enough. The land gave what was needed and people took it without fencing it off. No one had figured out yet how to want more than their share. Strength existed, but hadn't yet discovered that it could dispossess the weaker. The stars were visible every night because no one had built anything tall enough, or bright enough, to block them out.

I'm not making an argument for returning to that. I'm pointing at something it shows us about ourselves: that the baseline condition of a human life, before avarice arrived and reordered everything, was not scarcity. It was commonwealth. What avarice did — what it always does — was convert common wealth into private need. You can trace the whole history of poverty to that single move. Before it, there were no poor people. After it, there was nothing else.

> Avarice introduced poverty. By craving much, it lost all.

This is worth sitting with. The world didn't run short. People ran short of the willingness to share what the world provided. And having converted abundance into scarcity, they then spent generations trying to climb back to sufficiency through acquisition — which, of course, only deepens the original problem. Every estate that swallows its neighbor leaves two people worse off in the ways that matter: one who now has less than enough, and one whose soul has grown a little more cramped around what it owns.

Here's what philosophy actually does, in my understanding — and I want to be clear about this because I think it's easy to ask too much of it or too little. It didn't invent tools. It didn't teach us to bake bread or build ships or weave cloth. Those are the achievements of ingenuity, not wisdom, and there's no shame in that. Ingenuity is real. But ingenuity aimed at the wrong things, or ungoverned by any sense of what's actually worth wanting, is how you end up with marble floors and a clenched, anxious heart behind them.

What philosophy does is something harder to see and slower to achieve: it trains the mind to distinguish between what it needs and what it has merely learned to crave. It strips the false glamour from what fortune gives and takes away. It teaches you to hold good fortune lightly and bad fortune steadily, which turns out to be the same skill. It shows you that the person who needs nothing from chance is the freest person alive — not because they have everything, but because they've stopped requiring anything beyond what they already have within them.

Those early people, the ones who slept under open sky and watched the stars wheel overhead in silence — they weren't wise. They were innocent, which is different. They hadn't yet developed the capacity to sin in the full sense, which means they hadn't yet developed the capacity for virtue either. Virtue isn't the absence of temptation. It's what you become through choosing correctly, repeatedly, over time, in the presence of real alternatives.

But they had something we've largely traded away in exchange for our ceilings and our locks and our categories of property: a life lived at the scale nature actually intended. No house so large it required defending. No wealth so abstract it could no longer be felt. No poverty, because no one had yet thought to enclose the commons.

> Nature did not wish us to be harassed. For whatever she forced upon us, she equipped us.

We are the ones who made it complicated. And philosophy, at its best, is the long work of uncompleting what we've complicated — not by returning to the trees, but by recovering, inside the life we actually have, something of what those people had without trying: sufficiency, clear sight, and a kind of peace that doesn't depend on what fortune is doing this season.

That's what I want for you. Not the golden age. Just its deepest lesson, available to us still.
