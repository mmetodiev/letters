title: "What the Old Walls Know"
--- 

I've been spending time at a place with real history — not the performed kind, the kind that's been turned into a destination, but the quiet kind where you walk through rooms and feel the weight of who was here before you. It does something to your sense of proportion. The person who lived here made decisions that changed the course of things, and then stepped aside voluntarily when staying would have cost too much. Chose exile over compromising what he'd built. Left because he believed his country was worth more than his presence in it.

I keep thinking about that. The willingness to disappear when disappearing is the right thing. We don't see much of that now.

The building itself is modest to the point of austerity — stone walls, small windows, a bath barely bigger than a closet and almost entirely dark. The kind of place that by modern standards would be considered uninhabitable by anyone with options. And the man who bathed here had options. He simply didn't confuse options with requirements.

Compare that to what we build for ourselves now. The renovation plans, the finishes, the fixtures, the particular grade of stone for the particular room. I'm not immune to this — I notice what I notice, I want what I want. But standing in those old rooms you feel, briefly and clearly, how much of what we call comfort is actually restlessness in a more expensive form. We upgrade because we've stopped being able to feel what we already have.

> We have become so luxurious that we will have nothing but precious stones to walk upon. Luxury works out new devices, always to her own eventual undoing.

Here's what I didn't expect: the day's real lesson came not from the famous history but from the man who owns the estate now. He's a careful, attentive farmer, the kind who notices everything, and he spent an hour showing me how to transplant old olive trees. Trees that are decades gone, root systems established, seemingly fixed in their place — and he moves them. Not carelessly. With attention to the roots, the soil, the packing, the trimming back of what's old and dry to give the new growth room to take hold.

His point, which I'm still turning over: almost anything can be moved and reestablished, if you handle it with enough care.

He also showed me trees he'd planted that won't produce in his lifetime. Not as a complaint — as a fact, stated plainly, with something like satisfaction. He's planting for whoever comes after him. The tree will be shading someone he'll never meet.

I think about this in relation to the work we do on ourselves — the habits, the thinking, the slow effort to become someone more worth being. Some of it pays off quickly. Some of it takes years to show. And some of it — the deepest changes, the ones that go all the way down into character — those are for the people who come after us. The children who will grow up in whatever atmosphere we create. The friends who will be shaped by what we modeled. The part of us that continues after we're gone, in ways we can't fully control or predict.

Plant anyway. Trim back what's dry. Pack the soil carefully so the new roots can take hold.

The old walls are patient about this. They've seen it work before.
