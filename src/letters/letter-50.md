title: "The faults you blame on circumstance are travelling with you."
---

I heard something recently that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. A woman I know lost her sight — not gradually, but suddenly — and doesn't know it. She keeps asking to be moved to a brighter room. She thinks it's the light that's wrong. She has no idea the darkness is inside her.

I couldn't decide whether to laugh or to sit very still with it. Because that's all of us, isn't it. Different rooms, same blindness.

We tell ourselves the problem is the place, the timing, the people around us. That we'd be different somewhere else, in different circumstances, with a different set of constraints. And then we move, or the circumstances change, and we find ourselves making exactly the same mistakes in the new location. The faults follow. They always follow — because they're not attached to the place. They're attached to us.

> The evil that afflicts us is not external. It lives in our very vitals. And that is precisely why it is so hard to cure — we do not know we are diseased.

Here is what I want to offer, though, because I don't want to leave you only with the diagnosis. The work of changing is hard at the beginning — genuinely hard, in the way that anything unfamiliar to a frightened mind is hard. But it doesn't stay hard. This is the thing people don't tell you, or don't believe when they're told: the medicine, once it starts working, begins to feel good. Not like deprivation. Like relief.

Other cures — most cures — you endure until health returns and then you stop. This one is different. It becomes pleasurable as it heals. The clarity that comes from seeing yourself honestly, from dropping the excuses, from beginning to act in alignment with what you actually believe — that has its own quality of pleasure that nothing else resembles.

And don't believe that it's too late, or that the patterns are too old to change. Bent timber can be straightened with sustained heat. Wood that grew crooked can be shaped to what's needed. The soul is more pliable than any of that — more yielding, more responsive to patient and honest attention.

> Learning virtue means unlearning vice. And once virtue takes hold in the place that rightfully belongs to it, it does not leave easily.

The first steps are the hardest. Everything after that is maintenance, and then eventually, something that feels like home.

Yours, still in the process of seeing clearly,
