chapterNum: 93
title: "Not How Long, But How Much"
--- 

Someone I know is grieving a friend who died young, and part of the grief is the feeling that something was owed — more years, more time, more of whatever was still to come. I understand that feeling. I've had it myself. But I've been sitting with a different question lately: what exactly is it that we think more time would have provided?

If the life was full — if the person loved well, thought clearly, met their obligations, brought themselves honestly to the people and the work that mattered — then what does a longer version of the same life add? More of the same good is still just good. The completeness was already there. We mistake duration for substance because duration is easier to measure.

> Let us measure lives by their performance, not their duration. Let us see to it that our lives be noteworthy not for their width but for their weight.

I think about people I've known who lived long and essentially accomplished nothing with the time — not nothing in the biographical sense, but nothing in the sense of becoming who they might have been, of doing the interior work that a life is actually for. Eighty years in which the soul never really took hold of itself. Did those people live long? They existed long. The distinction matters. A tree exists. Living, in the sense I mean, requires something more.

Your friend who died young had completed something. The age was incomplete; the life was not. These are different things, and we conflate them because we've been taught to measure in years rather than in what the years contained.

I want to be honest about my own stake in this, because I notice I don't entirely believe it yet — not in my bones, the way I'd need to believe it if the test actually came. I still want more time. I still find myself thinking in terms of what's left to do, as if the value of my life were somewhere out ahead of me rather than in what I'm doing right now. That's the version of this I'm working on: treating each day as though it might be the last one, not morbidly, but with the clarity that scarcity produces. Not counting on tomorrow to do what today is for.

What I have is my own. Not the years still to come — those belong to fortune. What I have is this: the quality of my attention right now, the choices I'm making right now, the person I'm being in this moment rather than the person I plan to become when circumstances improve. That's what's actually mine. Everything else is on loan.

The fullest life isn't the longest one. It's the one that reaches wisdom — or gets close enough that the soul knows what it was reaching for. A person who has genuinely understood how to live, even briefly, has done something that no amount of additional years could improve on. They've completed the thing. A year added would simply be more of the same, and if what came before was good, more of the same is not nothing — but it's not what makes the life matter.

The question worth asking isn't *how long did they live?* It's *did they flourish?* And sometimes — not always, but sometimes — the answer to that question is yes, and the number of years is beside the point.

He still lives. That's the right way to say it.
