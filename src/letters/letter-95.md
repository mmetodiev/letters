title: "A Star to Steer By"
--- 

There's a difference between knowing what to do and knowing why — and the difference matters more than it sounds. A person who has been given good rules to follow will behave well right up until the moment the rules don't quite cover the situation, or the cost of following them becomes high enough that the rules alone can't hold. Then you find out whether there's anything underneath.

This is what I mean when I say that precepts need roots. Advice without principle is like a leaf without a branch — it will work for a while on stored energy, then wither. You need something deeper: a view of the whole, a settled sense of what matters and what doesn't, an understanding of what you're actually for. From that, the right action in any particular situation becomes something you can find rather than something you have to be told.

The sailor analogy is honest: you can tell a sailor to adjust the tiller, trim the sails, read the wind — and all of that is useful. But none of it tells him where he's going. For that he needs a star. And without a destination, all the skilled seamanship in the world is just competent wandering.

I think about how we live now — the genuine complexity of it, the number of forces pulling in different directions, the way that what everyone around you values shapes you before you notice it's happening. The old problems were simpler, and simpler remedies served. Now the corruption is more thorough, more ambient, more difficult to locate precisely because it's everywhere. You can't just remove the obvious vice and call it done. The whole orientation needs adjusting.

> It is not enough, when arranging a life as a whole, to give advice about details.

What I want for you — what I keep returning to in everything I write — is not a list of things to do or avoid. It's a settled view of what is actually good. Because once you have that, genuinely have it, not as a position you've adopted but as something you've understood all the way down, the specific cases become navigable. You know what you're measuring against. You can't be easily deceived about value when you've done the work of establishing what things are actually worth.

Here's the passage I keep returning to, and I want to leave it with you because it says something about this better than I can in my own words:

*All that you behold, that which comprises both god and man, is one — we are the parts of one great body. Nature produced us related to one another, since she created us from the same source and to the same end.*

This is the doctrine underneath all the precepts. Treat the shipwrecked sailor as you would want to be treated. Point the wanderer toward the right road. Share what you have with the person who has nothing. These instructions make sense only if you've first understood the thing they rest on: that the separation between you and other people is less absolute than it appears, that their suffering is not foreign to you, that the arch holds because the stones support each other.

Without that foundation, the instructions are just etiquette. With it, they become something you can't unknow.

And then there's Cato — who rises at the end of this letter like a figure out of smoke, someone I keep invoking not because he was perfect but because he understood exactly what he was for, and acted accordingly when the cost was absolute. He didn't waver when the whole state was in ruins around him. He didn't calculate which side was more likely to win. He had a principle, arrived at through long practice and honest thought, and he followed it to the end.

I'm not asking you to be Cato. I'm pointing at what it looks like when the star is real and the sailor has been following it long enough that when the storm comes, the course holds.

> Life without ideals is erratic. As soon as an ideal is to be set up, doctrines begin to be necessary.

Find yours. Not the one that sounds best, or the one that costs least. The true one. Then let everything else follow from it.
