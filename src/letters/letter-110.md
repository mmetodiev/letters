title: "The Smallness of What Dazzles"
--- 

I want to tell you about a moment that stayed with me, one I heard described years ago and have been turning over ever since.

A teacher I admired was invited to an elaborate dinner — the kind where the wealth on display would stun most people into silence. Embossed silver, imported goods, trained servants, every excess of a prosperous empire arranged for maximum effect. He sat through it. He watched. And when he left, he found that he wanted less than when he arrived.

Not because he'd delivered a verdict on the immorality of luxury. Not because he'd reminded himself of the right philosophical position on wealth. But because he'd looked at it closely — really looked — and seen how small it actually was. An evening's arrangement. A display designed to impress, not to satisfy. Something that filled a few hours and then was cleared away, no trace remaining except in the minds of those who'd briefly wanted what they saw.

> Such things are displayed, not possessed; while they please, they pass away.

This is the move I want you to practice: not the rejection of beautiful things, but the accurate perception of them. There's a kind of person who has trained themselves to despise luxury on principle — who takes pride in simple living as a form of discipline or as proof of character. I understand that impulse, but it's not quite right. You're not doing anything especially virtuous by disdaining a gold couch. The gold couch was never the point.

The point is whether you can see clearly what things actually are — their weight, their duration, their real effect on the life you're trying to live. Gold looks like wealth. A feast looks like happiness. Influence looks like power. None of these appearances survives close examination. You examine them not by rejecting them but by watching them: what happens to the elaborate dish once it goes down? What remains of the evening's splendor by morning? What does the person who owns all of this actually feel, underneath the performance of having it?

Most of what frightens or dazzles us has never been examined. We pass fear and desire from one person to another like something we caught and never questioned. Nobody has walked up to the thing they dread and looked at it directly. Nobody has stood in the room with what they want most and asked what it's actually made of. We run toward things and away from other things in a kind of permanent dusk, blundering at speed without ever stopping to see where we are.

What would it mean to stop? To actually look?

It would mean realizing that the necessities are easy — that what the body genuinely needs, nature has made available without much effort, that the elaborate machinery of wanting and acquiring is something we built on top of a much simpler foundation. The suffering is mostly in the machinery, not in the underlying life.

Here is the challenge that stays with me, the one I find myself returning to when the wanting gets loud: *we have water, we have what we need; let us compete in happiness with Jupiter himself.* And then the harder version: what if even that is more than we need? What if the freedom we're after isn't freedom from poverty but freedom from the whole structure of needing fortune's permission — any permission — to be all right?

That's the only freedom that holds when things go wrong. Not having enough, but having made yourself someone for whom enough is genuinely enough.

I don't say I've arrived there. But I know what the destination looks like, and I know that every clear-eyed look at what actually dazzles me is a step in the right direction.
