chapterNum: 98
title: "What Fortune Cannot Take Back"
--- 

Here is the thing about happiness that took me longest to understand: if it depends on conditions, it isn't happiness. It's a favorable condition. And favorable conditions change.

I don't mean this as a counsel of despair. I mean it as the most practical thing I know. The joy that enters from outside — from good news, good health, good fortune, the presence of people you love — is real while it lasts. But it carries within it the seed of its own instability, because it depends on things that can be taken. The joy that comes from inside, from having become someone whose foundation isn't made of what fortune is currently doing — that one holds. It increases, even. It's there when the conditions change, which they always do.

Fortune doesn't give us good or evil directly. She gives us raw material. What we make of it is ours.

I want to say something about anticipatory suffering, because I think it's where most of us spend more of our lives than we should. There's a particular kind of misery that arrives before the thing itself — the rehearsing of loss while the thing is still present, the inability to enjoy what you have because you're already grieving its eventual absence. I know this pattern well. The mind gets ahead of circumstances and starts suffering on credit.

> He suffers more than is necessary who suffers before it is necessary.

And here's the thing: it doesn't help. The advance suffering doesn't cushion the eventual blow. It just means you've paid twice — once in anticipation, once in fact. The grief you do before the loss doesn't reduce the grief after. It only steals the time in between, which was the time you actually had the thing you were afraid to lose.

But there's a sentence I keep returning to, one of the most quietly consoling things I know: *to have may be taken from us; to have had, never.*

Whatever you've loved and lost — a person, a time of life, a version of yourself — the having of it is permanent. It happened. Nothing that comes after can make it not have happened. The memory of it, the shaping it did, the way it made you who you are — these are not confiscated when the thing itself goes. Fortune can take the thing. She cannot take your having known it.

This is why I think gratitude is not just a virtue but a form of accuracy. Ingratitude after loss — treating what you had as though it counts for nothing because it's gone — is a kind of falsification. It was real. It mattered. Say so.

I'm writing this with someone specific in mind — someone old and in pain, who is facing what we all face eventually, and facing it with a steadiness that instructs me. He doesn't hurry toward death because he's tired of suffering, and he doesn't cling to life while dreading death. He simply holds both, and keeps going. I watch this and think: this is what the philosophy is actually for. Not for the argument. For the moment when the argument has to become something you live.

He has lived fully. Whatever comes next is surplus, and he knows it. There's a particular kind of freedom in that — in having already completed the essential thing, so that what remains is gift rather than obligation.

> The real good does not perish. It is certain and lasting. It is the only immortal thing that falls to mortal lot.

Hold onto what is yours. Let go of what was only ever borrowed. And be grateful — not as a performance, but as an honest accounting of what you actually received.
