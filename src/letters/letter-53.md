chapterNum: 53
title: "The worse the illness, the less you feel it."
---

I had a miserable journey recently — rough seas, seasickness so relentless I eventually forced the pilot to land somewhere, clambered off the boat into the water rather than wait, scrambled over rocks in my cloak just to get onto solid ground. Graceless and undignified and worth every bit of it. There is a point at which you cannot endure yourself, and the only thing left is to get out by whatever means is available.

Lying on the shore afterwards, waiting for my stomach to stop arguing with me, I found myself thinking about how thoroughly we hide our troubles from ourselves. Even the physical ones, which are right there, impossible to miss. We explain them away, rename them, reclassify them as something more minor. A pain in the joints becomes fatigue. A recurring ache becomes a sprain. Only when it swells beyond all possible denial do we finally call it what it is.

> The diseases of the soul work the same way — except worse. The more serious they are, the less we perceive them.

A light sleep is restless; you toss, you half-wake, you know dimly that you are dreaming. But deep sleep annihilates everything — you feel nothing, know nothing, have no perception of yourself at all. The soul's deepest faults work exactly like that. The person most in their grip is the last to know. Confession of a fault is itself a sign of soundness — you can only describe the dream after you've woken from it.

So the question is how to wake up. And I keep arriving at the same answer — the only one that has ever actually worked for me. Not willpower alone. Not good intentions. Not the resolution made on a good day that evaporates on a hard one. Philosophy — by which I mean the genuine, daily, unhurried practice of examining how you're living and whether it matches what you believe. That is the thing that can shake the deep sleep off.

But it only works if you give it real time. Not the leftover hours. Not the margins of a life organised around everything else. It is not a subject to be studied occasionally — it is the practice that makes the rest of life possible. If you were seriously ill you would stop everything and attend to it. This is serious. Attend to it.

> The wise man has one advantage over the gods — the gods are freed from fear by nature's bounty. The wise man is freed by his own.

That's the destination. Not the absence of difficulty — philosophy doesn't promise that. It promises something better: that when the blows come, they don't settle in you. That some you deflect, some you absorb without damage, and some you send back. The person who has done this work is not untouchable. They are just no longer breakable in the old ways.

What a strange and wonderful thing to aim for. The weaknesses of a human being and the serenity of something larger.

Yours, still drying off from the rocks,
