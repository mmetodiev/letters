title: "The Door Is Always Open"
---

There are places that bring you back to yourself. I drove past one recently — somewhere I spent a lot of time when I was younger — and for a moment I could feel the years stacked up between that person and this one. It wasn't sad, exactly. It was more like looking at a long voyage from the deck, watching the coastline disappear. You don't mourn the land. You're just aware of how far you've come, and how the direction only runs one way.

That's what I want to talk about. Not death as tragedy. Death as a door that was always there.


We tend to think of dying as something that happens *to* us — an interruption, a theft, the thing that ends the story too soon. But Seneca saw it differently, and I think he was right: the door was built into the house. You didn't earn the exit by suffering enough first. You don't have to wait to be ruined before you're allowed to leave. **The best thing life ever offered us was not one entrance but many exits.** That's not morbid. That's architecture.

I'm not saying give up easily. I'm saying: don't confuse clinging with courage.


Here's what I keep coming back to. He writes about a gladiator — a man with nothing, no status, no choices, no future — who found a way out anyway, through means I won't describe because they're genuinely awful. And Seneca doesn't flinch. He says: *look at what a human being will do to stay free.* The method was ugly. The will behind it was not.

I think about that when I hear people say they'd endure anything to stay alive. Maybe. But there's a difference between endurance and mere survival, between staying because your life still has meaning and staying because you're afraid of what it means to go. **Dying well isn't a privilege of the brave. It's available to anyone who decides that life is only worth living well.**


This isn't abstract for me. You know that. I've watched people I love stay too long — in lives that had stopped fitting, in bodies that had become prisons, in situations where "hope" was just another word for delay. And I've watched people leave with grace. The difference wasn't luck. It was whether they had decided, quietly and finally, that they were still the author, even at the end.

I want that for you. I want it for myself. Not the death — not yet, not for a long time — but the *posture*. The knowledge that the door is there. That you're not trapped. That you're staying because you *choose* to stay, not because you can't imagine the alternative.


That choice, held lightly, changes everything about how you live. It's not a morbid thought to carry. It's actually a freeing one. The person who knows they can leave, and stays anyway, is staying for real reasons. Every morning is a decision. Every year is something you built, not something that just happened to you.

Stay. I want you to stay. But stay like someone who knows the door, and turns away from it on purpose.

With all of it,
*M.*
