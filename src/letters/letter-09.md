title: "What You Can Carry Through Fire"
---

There's a question I want to settle, because it comes up whenever someone hears that the goal is a self-sufficient soul: does that mean we don't need anyone? Does the wise person retreat into themselves and dispense with friendship as an unnecessary dependency?

No. And the confusion matters enough to address directly.

The wise person is self-sufficient for happiness — not for existence. For mere existence, for the daily business of being alive, they need as much as anyone: hands, eyes, other people, the ordinary apparatus of a human life. What they don't need from outside is the good itself. That, they carry within. But carrying the good within doesn't make you indifferent to other people; it frees you to care about them properly, for their own sake rather than for what they provide you.

Here's the distinction: the person who seeks friends for utility will lose them the moment the utility runs out. A friendship begun for advantage will end when the advantage shifts — and it always shifts. But the person who wants friends in order to have someone to sit beside when they are sick, someone to ransom from captivity, someone to follow into exile — that person has understood what friendship actually is. You make a friend so that you have someone for whom you would die. Start from that, and the friendship will hold through anything.

Phidias, if he loses a statue, can carve another. The person skilled in friendship can replace a friend — not the person, who is irreplaceable, but the relationship, which is a capacity they carry and can offer again. This is not callousness. It is the opposite: because they don't need friendship to be happy, they can give themselves to it completely, without calculation, without the desperate clinging that ruins both parties.

The farmer who sows has a different pleasure than the farmer who reaps. The painter absorbed in painting is happier than the painter contemplating the finished canvas. **Making a friend, not having made one — that is the richer pleasure.** The work itself is the joy.

And then there's Stilbo. His city had been taken. His children and wife were gone. He emerged from the destruction alone, and when the conqueror asked what he had lost, he said: *I have all my goods with me.*

The conqueror suddenly wasn't sure he'd won anything.

This is what it means to be self-sufficient — not that you feel nothing when loss arrives, but that what you actually possess cannot be taken. Your goods travel with you. The fire cannot reach them. The sword cannot confiscate them. Fortune can rearrange everything external and leave the essential untouched, because the essential was never in the things she arranges.

What made you unhappy is not your condition but your opinion of it. *Unblest is he who thinks himself unblest.* This is not wishful thinking or the power of positive attitude. It is something deeper: that only the person who has genuinely built their life on what cannot be taken can say this and mean it. The fool is always troubled by weariness of itself; only the person who has done the work is pleased with what they have.

You can be that person. You're already working toward it.

I have all my goods with me. Carry yours.

Yours, through whatever fire,
