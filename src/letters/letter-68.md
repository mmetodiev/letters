title: "Retire, But Don't Announce It"
---

I want to talk to you about stepping back — not giving up, not disappearing, but the particular kind of withdrawal that's actually an advance. The kind where you leave a smaller room for a larger one, even if no one sees you go.

Don't make a performance of it. Don't tell people you're simplifying, or that you've seen through the machinery, or that you're done with things that used to matter. Just quietly stop. The person who announces their retreat is still playing to the room.

> Genuine withdrawal needs no placard. The ones who matter will notice the quality of your presence. Everyone else won't notice you're gone.

Here's what I'm actually trying to tell you: when you do pull back, use the space honestly. Don't fill it with self-congratulation about how much you've left behind. Use it to sit with yourself in the way you'd sit with someone you're trying to help — noticing what's actually wrong, not performing what you'd like to be true.

Seneca says he went into retirement not as a physician but as a sick man. I find that more trustworthy than almost anything else he writes. The wound he's tending isn't visible. It's somewhere in the chest. He can't show it, but he knows it's there, and he's trying, late in the day, to do something about it.

That's the work. Not the public version of wisdom — the private one. The conversation you have with yourself when no one's watching, where you're finally willing to hear what you actually think.

> It is not too late. But it is also not early. Pick up the pace accordingly.

I'm telling you this because I see you considering a version of stepping back that's still mostly for show. Go further in. Get quieter. Do the real thing.

Yours, from somewhere off the main road —
