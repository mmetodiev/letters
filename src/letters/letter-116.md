title: "Don't Negotiate With the Entrance"
---

I want to push back on something that sounds reasonable and isn't: the idea that the solution to strong feeling is moderate feeling. That grief moderated is healthy grief. That desire kept in check is fine desire. That anger, properly regulated, is acceptable.

It sounds wise. It isn't. A disease doesn't become healthy by being mild. The question isn't whether the fever is high or low — the question is whether you're sick.

Here's what I've observed, in myself more than anywhere: every emotion that eventually cost me something started small and manageable. It was easy to accommodate at first, easy to justify, easy to give a little ground to. Then it was harder to refuse, because I'd already let it in. Then it had made itself at home. The time to address it was at the door, not after it had settled into the furniture.

> It is more easy to forestall it than to forgo it.

A teacher I trust was once asked by a young man whether a wise person should fall in love. His answer was something like: the wise person is a separate question. For the rest of us — those who have not yet achieved the internal steadiness that genuine wisdom brings — we should be honest about what we're risking. Love disordered, uncontrolled, enslaved to another person's response: we know what this does. It makes us giddy when welcomed and humiliated when refused, and neither state is one we chose or can easily leave. This isn't an argument against love. It's an argument for knowing your own weakness before you step onto ice.

The same applies to everything we tell ourselves we can handle in moderation. We say we can drink without it becoming a habit, desire without it becoming need, grieve without it becoming the organizing principle of a year. Sometimes we're right. But we should be honest about how often we're wrong, and about the fact that the error is usually made at the beginning, when the choice still feels free.

Here's the thing I want you to hear, because I think it's the truest thing in this letter: we tell ourselves we can't achieve what we're actually just unwilling to try for. The inability is usually unwillingness dressed in a more sympathetic costume. We are attached to certain feelings, certain vices, certain patterns — not because they serve us but because they are ours, and giving them up feels like amputation.

But they were never load-bearing. They were parasites we'd mistaken for structure.

Nature gave us enough strength. What it didn't give us was the willingness to use it. That part is on us.

The reason is unwillingness. The excuse is inability. Know the difference.
