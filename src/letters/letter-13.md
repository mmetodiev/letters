chapterNum: 13
title: "Most of It Never Comes"
---

I want to tell you something I've learned from watching myself be afraid, which is this: the thing I feared was almost never as bad as the fear of it. And half the time, the thing never arrived at all.

This is not a counsel of optimism. I'm not telling you nothing bad will happen. I'm telling you that most of what occupies your dread right now is imagination, not reality — and imagination is a worse torturer than most of the things it conjures.

We exaggerate troubles. We anticipate them before they're due. We suffer from things that will never happen, and even when something is genuinely on its way, we run toward it rather than waiting for it to reach us. The suffering begins long before it needs to. That's the part we're actually choosing.

Here is the test I apply, and you might try it too: separate what is presently true from what might become true. Right now, in this moment — is there an actual harm? If your body is sound, if no specific injury has arrived, then your suffering is prospective. It exists in the future, which hasn't happened yet. How reliable is your evidence that it will? Rumors spread faster than truth. Fears multiply without constraint. We see a dust cloud raised by stampeding cattle and abandon camp.

For the future: it may come. But how often has the expected never arrived? How many disasters were averted at the last moment, how many swords checked at the throat? Even when something bad is genuinely coming, waiting for it in terror is still wasted time. It will be bad when it arrives. It needn't also consume the time before it arrives.

> We are tormented more often by things imaginary than by things real. We suffer more often in imagination than in reality.

And if you've weighed it honestly and still the fear wins — then say this to yourself, and mean it: *what if it does happen? Let us see who wins.* Socrates was made greater by the hemlock. Cato's death was his most eloquent speech. The worst arriving does not mean the self is defeated. It means the self is tested, and the self can pass the test.

I'm not asking you to be fearless. I'm asking you to be accurate. Examine what you fear instead of flinching from it. Most fears, when you walk up to them and look closely, are smaller than they appeared from a distance.

Here's today's thought, which I'm not naming the author of because they're obscure and the saying matters more than the name:

> The fool, with all his other faults, has this also — he is always getting ready to live.

Look around and you'll see this everywhere: people who are always in preparation, always arranging the conditions under which real life will begin. When the debt is cleared, when the children are grown, when the position is secured, when the health improves — then they'll begin. Watch long enough and you'll see them arrive at old age still getting ready.

The conditions will never all be right. They weren't right when you were young and they won't be right when you're old. The living is now, in whatever conditions exist now.

Begin.

Yours, in the present tense,
